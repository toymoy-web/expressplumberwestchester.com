What changes with a plumbing installation.

An installation has more planning than a repair. Pipe sizes, vent routes, drain slope and shutoff placement all need to be decided before drywall goes up. A fixture moved a few feet can mean opening a floor or wall for the drain.

Planning this early with a plumber saves costly changes during the rest of the remodel.

Rough-in, then finish: the two phases of new plumbing.

New plumbing happens in two stages. The rough-in puts supply lines, drains and vents inside open walls and floors before they are covered. The finish stage sets fixtures, connects them and tests everything after drywall and flooring are done. Inspectors usually look at the rough-in before the walls close, which is why timing matters.

If you are remodeling, tell the plumber the finished floor height, the tile thickness and the cabinet depth. A vanity drain that lands a half inch off can mean reopening a wall, and that is an easy mistake to avoid with one measurement.

  • Which fixtures are you installing, and do you have them on site?

  • Does the work need a permit, and who pulls it?
  • Will walls or floors be opened, and who restores them?
  • Are there timing limits from other trades?

Permits in West Chester Borough and the townships.

West Chester Borough's plumbing code requires a permit for plumbing work and a license for the plumber doing it. For a new bathroom or relocated fixture, expect the borough to issue the permit to a master plumber, with an owner exception only for personal work on a single-family home. Each surrounding township runs its own office, so ask the one that covers the address.

Why measurements matter before the plumber arrives.

  • The rough-in dimensions printed on the fixture box, which set where drains and valves must sit.
  • The distance from the wall to the center of the toilet drain, commonly twelve inches but not always.
  • The slope available for a drain run, because drains need a steady fall to carry waste.
  • The vent route, since every fixture needs air to drain properly.
  • The location of the main shutoff and the water heater, which affects how supply lines are routed.

Bring the plumber the spec sheets for any fixtures you have bought. Matching the plan to the real fixtures costs less than adapting plumbing afterward.

Scheduling around other trades.

A plumbing install rarely happens alone. Framing, electrical and sometimes HVAC all share the same walls. Plumbers usually come in after framing for the rough-in, then again after drywall, tile and cabinets for the finish. Giving the plumber the project calendar, even as a rough outline, prevents the common problem of a finish visit scheduled before the countertop is in.

If you are the general contractor on your own remodel, ask the plumber how long each visit will take and what must be finished before they arrive. A tile floor that is not yet sealed, or a vanity that is not yet set, can force a return trip.

What to do before new plumbing work starts.

Locate the main shutoff, clear work areas and confirm who pulls the permit. Installations follow the plumbing code the borough or township enforces and the fixture manufacturer's installation guidelines.

New fixtures in East Bradford are permitted through the township office on Copeland School Road, not the borough. Ask the plumber who will file before the rough-in is scheduled.

Renovations in the Downtown Historic District involve buildings from 1789 through the 1930s, so new fixtures often connect to old drains. Ask the plumber who is pulling the permit before the rough-in begins.

New fixtures, bathrooms and new construction.

Plumbing installation covers new fixtures, bathroom tub and shower installs, and rough-in work for new construction. Plan the fixture list and layout first.

For new construction, bring plans and ask for the rough-in and finish stages to be scheduled separately.
