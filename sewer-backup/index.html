What to do when sewage comes up.

1

Stop all water use.

No flushing, laundry, dishwashers or showers. Every gallon needs somewhere to go.

2

Keep people and pets out.

Sewage carries bacteria and viruses. Keep children away from the area.

3

Protect yourself.

Wear gloves and boots if you must be near it, and wash hands thoroughly.

4

Call, then document.

Photograph the damage for your insurer and call the 24/7 line.

Where the sewage goes first, and why.

In a backup, wastewater rises to the lowest opening in the house. That is usually a basement floor drain, a laundry sink, a shower in a lower bathroom or a toilet on the lowest level. If your lowest fixture is the one overflowing and upstairs drains still work, the blockage is in the main line downstream of your house branch.

That detail helps the plumber. It tells them whether to look for a blockage inside the house, in the lateral between the house and the street, or at the connection to the public main.

Your line or the municipal main.

Likely your private line.

  • Only your house is affected.
  • Neighbors have normal drainage.
  • The backup began in the lowest fixture.
  • Roots, grease or a collapsed lateral are suspected.

Possibly the municipal main.

  • Neighbors report the same backup.
  • It happens during or right after heavy rain.
  • Sewer smells are strong in the street.
  • Several houses on your block are affected.

If you suspect the main, call the municipality's public works office as well as a plumber, and tell the dispatcher so the camera can check the lateral.

Storms and sewer backups.

Heavy rain can send stormwater into aging sewer laterals through cracks and open joints, and can overload municipal systems. During the remnants of Ida in September 2021, Chester County Emergency Services answered more than 4,000 calls within ten hours. A backup that appears only after big storms deserves a camera inspection of the lateral.

Ask your insurer about a water backup endorsement, because standard homeowners policies often exclude sewer and drain backup.

Cleanup after a sewage backup.

Cleanup is its own job, and getting it wrong creates a health risk.

Material.Usual approach.Why.
Hard floors and concrete.Clean and disinfect.Nonporous surfaces can be sanitized.
Carpet and padding.Remove and discard.Porous materials cannot be fully cleaned.
Drywall near the floor.Cut out and replace.It wicks contaminated water upward.
Upholstered furniture.Usually discard.Cannot be disinfected reliably.
Wood furniture and cabinets.Clean and disinfect if sound.Depends on exposure time.

Protective gear matters: waterproof boots, gloves, eye protection and a mask. Children, pets and anyone with a weakened immune system should stay out of the area until it is cleaned.

Why backups often happen at the worst moment.

Sewer backups cluster when the system is stressed. Heavy water use, such as laundry, a full tub and a dishwasher at once, adds flow to a partly blocked line. A storm adds inflow from the ground. A holiday gathering puts many people through the same bathrooms in one evening.

If you have had one slow-drain warning in the past month, treat it as the last easy chance to clear the line before the next surge. That is also when a camera costs least compared to the cleanup it can prevent.

What to do with sewage in the house.

Keep people and pets out, stop all water use and wear boots and gloves if you must enter. Cleanup follows public health guidelines, and porous materials soaked by sewage are normally discarded.

When a backup follows a creek-flood storm, the cause can be stormwater entering the lateral, which is one of the themes on the East Bradford page. Tell the dispatcher if neighbors are affected too.

A backup in a rowhouse on Gay Street may involve a shared lateral, so ask the neighbors whether they have the same problem. Stop using water in the house and tell the dispatcher what the neighbors report.
