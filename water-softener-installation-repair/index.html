Test first.

How hard is your water, really?

Hardness varies from house to house depending on the source, so guessing leads to an oversized or undersized softener. Aqua Pennsylvania's 2025 report for the West Chester system names Brandywine Creek and two wells as the sources and does not list a hardness figure, so a test is the reliable route. A simple strip kit gives a rough value and a lab or professional test gives a more accurate one.

Homes outside the public system, on private wells permitted by the Chester County Health Department, should test their well water directly.

Sizing a softener without over-buying.

A softener's capacity is measured in grains it can remove between regenerations. Sizing comes from two numbers: how hard the water is, in grains per gallon, and how many gallons the household uses in a few days. A softener that is too small regenerates constantly and wastes salt. One that is too large may sit with water that goes stale between cycles.

A rough method is to multiply the number of people by about 75 gallons a day, multiply by the hardness, and choose a unit whose capacity covers several days of that figure. Your plumber can run the numbers once the hardness test is done.

Softener or filter?

A softener swaps calcium and magnesium for sodium so scale does not form. It does not remove chlorine, lead, PFAS or other contaminants. If your concern is taste or a specific contaminant, a water filtration system addresses that. Many homes use both.

Protecting appliances.

A softener helps protect the water heater, so if your tank is failing early, check hardness before buying another one. Water heater replacement options and softener installation are often done in the same visit.

Whatever salt you use, check the tank monthly. Hard-pan salt bridges, where a crust forms above empty space, are a leading reason owners think the softener has failed.

Where the softener drain goes.

Every softener sends brine and rinse water to a drain when it regenerates. The drain line needs an air gap so wastewater cannot flow back into the unit, and it should go to a standpipe, floor drain or laundry sink, not directly into a sealed drain pipe.

If the softener is in a basement below the sewer level, a small condensate or sump pump may be required. Ask the plumber where the water will go before agreeing on a location, because the drain path decides where the unit can sit.

What to do if the unit leaks or overflows.

Move the bypass valve to bypass, unplug the control head, and mop up salt water, which is slippery and corrodes metal. Softener capacity ratings are published according to industry test standards, so compare models on the same basis.

Softener repairs, valves and brine tanks.

A softener that stops softening may have a valve, resin or control fault. Hard water signs include scale on fixtures and spotted glassware.

Brine tank and overflow problems, valve replacements and reinstallation after a move are all common service jobs. Tell the plumber the brand and model.
