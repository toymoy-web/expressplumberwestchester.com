Backflow Testing & Prevention in West Chester, PA.

Backflow is water flowing the wrong way, from a place it should not be back into the drinking water supply. Devices prevent it, and some need regular testing.

Symptom-first.

Why does my water supplier want a backflow test?

Plumbing that connects to something other than drinking water, such as an irrigation system, a boiler feed or a fire sprinkler line, can in rare conditions let that water flow backward. A backflow preventer is the valve that stops this, and many suppliers require the more complex types to be tested on a schedule.

If you received a notice asking for a test, it is about protecting the public supply, not about a problem inside your house.

What backflow protection actually protects.

Public water arrives under pressure, and pressure normally pushes water in one direction. When a main breaks or a fire hydrant draws heavily, pressure on your side can briefly drop below pressure in a connected system. That is called back-siphonage. The other cause, back-pressure, happens when a boiler, pump or a tank sitting above the line pushes water against the supply.

In either case, whatever is on the downstream side can be drawn back toward the supply. A lawn sprayer sitting in a puddle of fertilizer or a boiler loop with treatment chemicals are the classic examples. The device exists to make that impossible, and testing proves it still works.

Irrigation and exterior spigots are part of everyday fixes for valves, pressure and leaks. Commercial properties often need testing as part of commercial plumbing service.

Getting ready for a test visit.

1

Find the device.

It is usually a brass assembly with two or three test cocks, near the meter, on the irrigation feed or in a vault. Photograph it and note any tag.

2

Clear access.

Trim back plants and remove anything blocking the device. A crew cannot test an assembly buried in a shrub.

3

Expect a short shutdown.

Water to the connection is turned off for a short time during the test. Plan around laundry, irrigation or business hours.

4

Keep the report.

Whoever requires the test needs the paperwork. Keep a copy with your records for the next cycle.

A failed test is common on devices that have not been touched in years, and the fix is often a rebuild kit rather than a new assembly.

Installing a new device.

New backflow devices are required in some cases when a new irrigation system, a fire line or a commercial connection is added. The right device depends on the hazard. Water with fertilizer or chemicals needs stronger protection than clean water in a fire line.

Installation usually involves a permit and an inspection, and the device must be placed where it can be tested, with clearance around it, and not below grade where it could flood. Ask the plumber how the chosen location meets the supplier's requirements.

What to do if you suspect contamination.

If water from a tap looks, smells or tastes wrong after a pressure drop or a main break, stop drinking it and call your supplier. Backflow assemblies are tested according to the supplier's requirements and the testing standards the tester is certified under, so ask which apply to your connection.

A leaking test cock or relief port is a plumbing fault in its own right, and burst pipe repair applies if a line has split.

Offices and campus buildings in West Goshen are more likely than houses to have testable assemblies. Ask your supplier or the township office whether yours needs a yearly report.

Restaurants, the hospital campus and the university buildings use large, high-demand water systems where backflow devices are common. Ask who keeps the annual test record for a commercial property.

Annual backflow tests and replacements.

Many properties with irrigation, fire lines or commercial service require an annual backflow assembly test. Assemblies that fail may be repaired or replaced.

If you manage several units, ask for them to be tested in one visit and keep the test reports for your water authority.
