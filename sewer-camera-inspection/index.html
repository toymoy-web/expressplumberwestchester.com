What a camera shows that cleaning cannot.

Cleaning clears a blockage. A camera tells you why the blockage formed. It shows roots entering a joint, a low spot where water sits, a crack, a separated connection or a build-up of scale on iron pipe.

That information decides whether the next step is routine cleaning, a spot repair or a bigger project.

What the camera crew needs from you.

A good inspection starts with an access point. The best is a two-way cleanout, a capped pipe near the foundation that lets the camera enter in the direction of the street. If the house lacks one, a toilet can be pulled, or the camera can enter through a roof vent, though both are slower and see less.

Tell the crew about any history: backups, recent roots, previous repairs, neighbors with problems. History shapes where the camera slows down, and where it checks the connection with the public main.

Buying a home near West Chester.

Buyers are often advised to include a sewer scope in their inspection, because lateral repairs are the owner's cost and a failing line can be expensive. The recording gives you a firm number to negotiate with and a baseline for future work.

Pennsylvania law requires an 811 call before any digging that follows, so a camera scope also helps plan the dig.

Related services.

If the footage shows buildup, hydro jetting cleans it. If it shows damage, sewer line repair options compares options, including trenchless pipe lining.

Reading the footage like a buyer.

  • Ask for the distance to each defect, measured from the access point, so the problem can be located above ground.
  • Look at the pipe material the camera identifies, since cast iron, clay and plastic fail in different ways.
  • Note any area where water sits. A shallow puddle can be normal after a flush, and a deep pool points to a sag.
  • Ask whether the camera could reach the connection to the municipal main, and what it showed there.
  • Request a written summary that lists each finding and your options for repair.

If a seller refuses a scope, that tells you something too. A line you cannot see is a risk you cannot price.

How often to scope a sewer line.

There is no fixed schedule. A line in good condition with no history of problems may only need a scope when you buy the house or when symptoms appear. A line with a history of roots may be worth scoping every year or two to see whether they are returning.

After any repair, a follow-up scope confirms that the work did what it was supposed to do. Keep the recordings together with the repair invoice, because they show the line's condition at a point in time.

What to do before an inspection.

Make sure the cleanout is reachable and keep water use light so the pipe is not full on arrival. Reports should state the pipe material, distances and defects, according to the inspector's recorded footage.

A buyer looking at a house near the borough line in West Goshen can order the scope during the inspection period. The township page lists the sewer office to ask about the connection.

A buyer looking at an older house near Marshall Square or the courthouse blocks is looking at a lateral that may be decades old. A camera inspection before closing shows the pipe material, the slope and any root intrusion.
