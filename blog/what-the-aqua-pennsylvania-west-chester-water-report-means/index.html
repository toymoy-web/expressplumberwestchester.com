What the Aqua Pennsylvania West Chester Water Report Means for Your Filter Choice.

Every year Aqua Pennsylvania publishes a water quality report for the West Chester system. Most people never read it. This is a plain-English walk through what it says and what it leaves out.

Written by the West Chester Express Plumbing team. Published 1 October 2026. Updated 1 October 2026.

Where the water comes from.

Aqua Pennsylvania's 2025 Water Quality Report for the West Chester Division says most of the drinking water comes from the Brandywine Creek and two wells. A source water assessment for Brandywine Creek found a high susceptibility to potential spills on highway corridors and a moderate susceptibility to wastewater discharges, including potential impacts from nitrates and taste and odor compounds.

That is a statement about risk to the source, not about what comes out of your tap. Treatment sits in between, and the report's detected-contaminant tables describe the result.

What these numbers do and do not tell you.

The report describes water as it leaves the system and at sampled homes. It does not describe your pipes. Lead can come from fixtures and solder inside a house, even when the utility's samples are clean. Copper tests at the tap measure corrosion in home plumbing, and a result of 0.04 ppm is low.

Hardness is not published in the report. If you want to know how hard your water is, a test strip or a professional test gives a number for your tap. That number decides whether a softener makes sense.

Which concern points to which equipment.

  • Chlorine taste and odor: activated carbon at the kitchen tap or the main.
  • Sediment or rust particles: a sediment filter at the main.
  • A concern about lead from old fixtures: a filter certified to NSF/ANSI 53 for lead.
  • PFAS reduction: a reverse osmosis system certified to NSF/ANSI 58, or another system independently certified for the specific compound.
  • White scale on fixtures: a softener, not a filter.

Choosing between filter and softener.

A water filtration system removes contaminants and improves taste. A water softener removes hardness minerals. They solve different problems, and many homes use both.

If scale is your complaint, test hardness first. If taste or a specific contaminant is your concern, choose a filter certified for it.

In Westtown and the other townships around the borough, some homes are on the public system and some on private wells. A well is tested by its owner, with guidance from the Chester County Health Department, and the Aqua report does not apply.

Reading the next report.

Aqua publishes the report each year, and numbers change. Compare year to year rather than reading one report alone. If the detected list grows, or a violation appears, the report's contact line, 877-987-2782, is the place to ask questions.

How to read the table in the report.

Water quality reports use a few terms that look intimidating. The MCL, or maximum contaminant level, is the highest amount of a contaminant allowed in drinking water. The MCLG, or goal, is the level at which no known health risk exists, and it is often zero for contaminants like lead. The action level is a trigger for treatment or other steps if exceeded in too many samples, as with lead and copper at the tap.

The 90th percentile means that nine in ten of the sampled homes had results at or below that value. Aqua's 2025 report shows 32 homes sampled for lead and copper, with none above the action level. Units matter too. Parts per million and parts per billion differ by a factor of one thousand, and parts per trillion are smaller still.

Units you will meet in the report.

Unit.Plain meaning.Example.
ppm (parts per million).One part in a million.One drop in about 13 gallons.

ppt (parts per trillion).One part in a trillion.One drop in about 13 million gallons.

Should the PFAS line change what you buy?

The report lists voluntary PFAS sampling at entry points: PFBS at 2.5 to 2.8 parts per trillion, and no detection of GenX, PFHxS or PFNA. PFAS is a large family of chemicals, and different compounds have different health guidance and different limits. The report does not list PFOA or PFOS in that table, so look at the utility's PFAS page, referenced in the report, for the fuller picture.

If you want to reduce PFAS at home, the usual choices are reverse osmosis or a carbon system certified for the compounds you care about. Certification matters more than marketing. Ask for the listing, and read what it says the system is certified to remove.

What the report cannot tell you.

A utility report describes the water it delivers and a sample of taps. It cannot describe the pipes and fixtures inside your house, the age of your water heater, or whether your plumbing includes old lead solder. It does not cover private wells, which are the owner's responsibility. And it does not say whether your water tastes the way you like.

If any of those matter to you, a home test kit or a certified lab can test your own tap. Many people test the kitchen cold tap after letting the water sit overnight to see the worst case for lead and copper from the house plumbing.

What to do if you are worried about lead at your tap.

Run the cold tap for a few minutes after the water has sat overnight, and use cold water for drinking and cooking. Ask Aqua at 877-987-2782 about testing, and consider a filter certified to NSF/ANSI 53 for lead. A plumber can check whether your own supply lines or fixtures are the source.
