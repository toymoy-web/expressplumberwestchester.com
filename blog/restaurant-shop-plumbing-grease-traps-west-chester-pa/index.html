Grease Traps and Floor Drains for West Chester, PA Shops.

In a restaurant or a busy shop, a backed-up drain is not just a plumbing problem. It is lost sales. A few routine steps keep the lines moving.

Written by the West Chester Express Plumbing team. Published 1 October 2026. Updated 1 October 2026.

Why grease is the enemy of a commercial drain.

Cooking grease is liquid when hot and solid when it cools. It enters the drain warm, travels a few feet and then sticks to the pipe wall, building a layer that narrows the pipe. Soap and food solids bind to it, and the pipe closes gradually until one busy evening it stops completely.

A grease interceptor, sometimes called a grease trap, sits in the drain line and slows the water so grease floats to the top and can be removed. It protects the building's pipes and the public sewer.

Floor sink.Receives indirect waste from equipment.Air gaps and cleaning.
Cleanout.Gives access to the line.Clear access and caps.
Backflow preventer.Protects the water supply from contamination.Annual testing where required.

A routine that prevents most backups.

  • Scrape plates and pans into the trash before washing, never into the sink.
  • Keep floor drain grates in place and clear of mop debris.
  • Pour water down rarely used floor drains monthly to keep the trap seal full.
  • Schedule interceptor cleaning on a calendar, not when it overflows.
  • Train staff to report slow drains early, since a slow drain is the last warning.
  • Keep a record of cleaning and plumbing service for inspectors and the landlord.

Floor drains and sewer gas.

A floor drain that is rarely used can dry out, and then it loses the water seal that blocks sewer gas. A musty or sewer smell in a back room is often a dry trap. Pouring water in solves it, but a drain that smells again soon after may have a venting or leak problem.

Floor drains can also be the first place a main line backup shows up. If the drain in the kitchen or basement of a shop starts to gurgle or rise, stop adding water and call. The cause may be in the building's lateral, not the drain itself.

Keeping the lines clean on a schedule.

For a busy kitchen, scheduled commercial plumbing service, including periodic jetting, is cheaper than emergency calls. A camera pass shows buildup before it blocks, and the interval can be adjusted to the kitchen's real use.

Backflow testing belongs on the same calendar. Devices on dishwashing equipment, boiler feeds and irrigation often need annual testing, and the reports must be filed with the supplier or the municipality.

West Goshen and the commercial mix.

West Goshen Township has restaurants, car dealerships, offices and corporate buildings along its main roads, as well as university buildings near the borough line. That mix means a plumber working there sees commercial problems daily. Chester County also licenses and inspects food establishments through its health department, and a kitchen that cannot keep its drains clear is likely to face questions. Routine service is the cheap way to avoid them.

What fails first when a kitchen is busy.

The weakest points in a commercial kitchen line are predictable. The pre-rinse sink near the dish station sends grease and food scraps into a branch that may be small. Floor drains near the cooking line collect hot grease from spills. And the main line, which serves everything, runs through a section of pipe the restaurant may share with neighbors.

Backups start at the lowest opening, usually a floor drain or a mop sink. If the floor drain begins to bubble or fill during service, the cause is generally downstream of that drain. Stop adding water, protect the floor and call. Pouring more water on a surging line only moves the sewage into the dining area faster.

Training and routine matter more than equipment.

Most commercial backups trace back to a habit. Fryer oil is poured into a floor drain at closing. Food scraps are rinsed into a sink without a strainer.

A grease interceptor goes uncleaned for months because nobody owns the task. Writing a short routine and assigning one person to check the interceptor lid and the floor drains weekly costs nothing and prevents most of these.

Keep a log with dates, who checked, and what was found. If an inspector asks, or if a landlord disputes responsibility, the log is the evidence that the kitchen did its part.

What property owners should keep on file.

  • As-built drawings or sketches showing where the main, the cleanouts and the interceptor are.
  • The dates and results of every grease interceptor pumping.
  • Backflow device test reports for each assembly on the property.
  • Contact details for the plumber and the pumping company.
  • A copy of the lease section that assigns plumbing responsibility.

Cleaning an interceptor and what the pumper should leave behind.

An interceptor is cleaned by pumping out the grease layer, the water and the settled solids, then scraping the baffles and walls and refilling the unit with clean water. A pumper who only skims the top and leaves the solids behind is not doing the job. Ask for a receipt that lists the date, the volume removed and where the waste was taken.

Many municipalities and authorities set the maximum interval between cleanings, and some require records. Even where nobody checks, the real test is simple: if the layer of grease reaches a quarter of the depth of the unit, it is time.

What to do if a kitchen drain backs up during service.

Stop all dishwashing and sink use, and keep staff away from wet floors near cooking equipment. Close off the affected area, put down absorbent material, and call for a plumber. Do not pour chemical cleaners into a standing drain, because the next person to work on it has to handle that water.
