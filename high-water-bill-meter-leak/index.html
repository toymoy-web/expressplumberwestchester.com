Read the bill before you chase the leak.

Start with the usage line, not the dollar total. A bill can rise because the billing period was longer, because a read was estimated and then corrected, or because rates changed. Compare the gallons or units for the same month last year. If usage is up by a third or more with nothing else changed, a leak becomes the likely explanation.

Look for the date the jump began. A sudden step up points to something that broke on a specific day, such as a toilet flapper, a hose left running or a pipe that split. A slow climb over several bills points to a growing leak or a worn fixture getting worse.

Questions for the utility.

Aqua Pennsylvania's customer line is 877-987-2782. If your meter is moving but you cannot find the leak, ask the utility whether a leak adjustment is available and what documentation it wants. A plumber's written findings, with date and location of the leak, are often useful for that conversation.

Seasonal changes matter too. Filling a pool, watering a new lawn or a house full of guests can all raise a bill without any leak.

Small fixes that often explain a big bill.

These are the most common culprits, and most take minutes to check.

  • Put dye in each toilet tank, wait twenty minutes without flushing, and look for color in the bowl.
  • Walk the outside of the house and check every spigot, hose connection and irrigation zone for dripping or soft ground.
  • Look at the water heater's temperature and pressure relief valve discharge pipe for any trickle.
  • Check whether a water softener is regenerating too often or stuck in a cycle that sends water to drain.
  • Look under every sink and behind the washing machine for damp shutoff valves or hoses.

If every one of these is clear and the meter still moves with everything off, the leak is hidden. That is when a plumber with detection equipment saves you from guessing, and when the written findings help you ask the utility about an adjustment.

When the leak is already fixed but the bill is still high.

A bill can stay high for one cycle after a repair because utilities bill for the period that included the leak. Look at the next full cycle for the real result. If usage has not come down, a second leak may exist, and the meter test is worth repeating.

Keep the plumber's invoice and the date of the repair with the bill that included the leak. If the utility offers an adjustment, those two documents are usually what it asks for.

What to do while you check for a leak.

Close the main if you hear water running with everything off and keep away from wet electrical areas. Water meters are tested to accuracy standards, so a moving dial with all fixtures off points to a leak, not a meter error.

A meter that moves with everything off in Westtown points to a leak after the meter, and the township page has a seasonal list of places to check. Start with toilets and outdoor taps.

A student rental near campus can show a high bill when a toilet runs unnoticed during a quiet week. Read the meter with every fixture off, then again an hour later, and report the difference.
